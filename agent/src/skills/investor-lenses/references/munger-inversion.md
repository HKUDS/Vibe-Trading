# Inversion Lens

> **Framework, not investment advice.** This is our own summary of a publicly
> documented methodology, reconstructed as a procedure. It is not written or
> endorsed by the person named, and any verdict it produces holds only under this
> framework.

## What this lens optimizes for

Avoiding permanent capital loss and avoidable stupidity. It never produces a buy
signal — its only outputs are "this specific failure path is live" and "these are
the observable triggers". Treat it as a veto layer stacked on a positive lens, not
a standalone decision procedure.

## Market-fit note

- Portable everywhere because it consumes reasoning rather than data.
- In markets where disclosure is thin, the inversion is *more* valuable, because
  the failure paths are the part you can reason about without a data feed.
- In A-shares and HK, add two failure families that US-designed checklists often
  omit: policy reversal (industry-level administrative intervention) and
  controlling-shareholder pledge/extraction risk.

## Priority signals (walk in this order)

1. **State the thesis as a falsifiable sentence.** "X compounds earnings at 15% for
   five years because of Y." If the thesis cannot be stated this way, invert the
   vagueness itself: an unfalsifiable thesis is the first failure path.
2. **Enumerate at least five distinct death paths**, drawn from different families,
   not five versions of one worry. Standard families:
   - demand disappears (substitution, generational shift, end-market decline)
   - supply economics invert (capacity, input costs, commoditization)
   - the balance sheet breaks (refinancing, covenants, pledged shares, currency)
   - the rules change (regulation, tariffs, licensing, sanctions, tax)
   - the people fail (fraud, succession, empire-building acquisitions, dilution)
   - the price was the whole thesis (multiple compression alone)
3. **For each path: probability band, time-to-impact, and a concrete trigger.** A
   trigger is an observable — a covenant ratio, a filing, a price, a policy
   document — not a feeling. Paths without triggers are not usable.
4. **Compute the loss under the worst plausible path**, not the average path. What
   does a shareholder recover in liquidation or restructuring? Is permanent loss of
   capital possible, or only volatility?
5. **Ask why intelligent, informed sellers are selling.** Construct the strongest
   version of the bear case as its holder would state it. If you cannot state it
   convincingly, you do not yet understand the position.
6. **Run the standing stupidity checklist** — the list of errors that recur
   independently of the specific company:
   - incentive-caused bias (whose compensation depends on this being true?)
   - social proof (how much of the conviction is that respected others hold it?)
   - commitment and consistency (are you defending a published view?)
   - availability (is the reasoning driven by the most recent vivid event?)
   - liking (is the founder's charisma doing analytical work?)
   - deprival super-reaction (are you acting to avoid missing out?)
7. **Historical analogues.** Name two companies that reached this same stage and
   failed, and state which of your death paths killed them.

## Disqualifiers (hard vetoes)

- **A failure path exists whose outcome is permanent total loss and whose trigger
  is not observable in advance.** Unhedgeable, unmonitorable, ruinous — decline.
- **The thesis requires a specific person to keep behaving well** with no structural
  constraint on them.
- **Leverage plus a variable outside management's control** (rates, a single
  customer, a single regulator) that can force action at the worst moment.
- **You cannot articulate the bear case.** Not a judgement about the company — a
  judgement about your readiness.
- **Any path whose probability you rate "low" purely because you have not
  investigated it.** Unknown is not low.

## Scoring rubric

There is no score. The output is a table:

| Death path | Family | Probability | Time to impact | Observable trigger | Recovery if it happens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Plus a single verdict line: *survivable*, *survivable only if monitored via the
listed triggers*, or *not survivable — decline*.

## Typical misuse

- **Used as a decision procedure on its own.** Every asset has failure paths; a
  lens that only enumerates risk will reject everything if allowed to be the whole
  process. It is a veto layer.
- **Symmetric pessimism.** Listing five paths for the candidate and none for the
  alternative (including cash, which has its own path: inflation and opportunity
  cost).
- **Untriggered risk theatre.** Long lists of unfalsifiable worries that no
  observation can ever retire. If a risk cannot be monitored or resolved, say so
  and treat it as a permanent haircut, not a bullet point.
- **Confusing volatility with permanent loss.** The lens is about impairment, not
  drawdown. Conflating them causes exit at exactly the wrong point.
- **Post-hoc inversion.** Running it after the position is on, when the commitment
  bias it is meant to detect is already operating.

## Falsifiers

- A listed trigger fires and the outcome does not follow → the causal model behind
  that path was wrong; revise the path rather than quietly dropping it.
- The bear case turns out to rest on a fact that is checkable and false → that path
  retires, and the retirement should be recorded with its evidence.
- A path you rated low-probability occurs at a peer → re-rate the family, not just
  the instance.

## Output contract

Report under the skill-level output contract. State the verdict as "under an
inversion framework, …", deliver the death-path table with triggers, and separate
permanent-loss risk from volatility. This is analysis, not investment advice.
